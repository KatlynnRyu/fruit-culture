2.5

Cultural Scripts and Adolescent Wellbeingguiones culturales de la emoción

Emotional expression is not just personality, it is taught. Hochschild (1979) named the unwritten "feeling rules" that govern which emotions we're expected to show, and Röttger-Rössler et al. (2015) showed that communities actively train children in which emotions to express and which to suppress. Our three cultures teach three distinct scripts:

Mexico & Latin America
warm positivity

Collectivistic, but organized around simpatía, warm, open expression of positive emotion and harmony, and familismo, the closeness of family life (Salvador et al., 2020).

United States
open expression

Individualistic cultures endorse greater emotional expressivity, a 32-country survey of 5,000+ people (Matsumoto et al., 2008).

Korea
restraint

Children learn to adjust or suppress emotion by social context, nunchi, chemyon, humility, deflecting compliments.

These differences track measurable gaps in how adolescents fare. Mexican adolescents in earlier PISA rounds reported life satisfaction above the OECD average, while in PISA 2022 Korean fifteen-year-olds reported 6.36, ranking 66th of 73 countries. Cultural scripts are only one factor among many, but a culture that gives young people a language for what they feel may offer more outlets than one that teaches them to hold it in.

“If you feel like you want to cry, just cry.”Student E · Guatemalan American

This thread ran clearly through our interviews: twelve of fifteen participants, across Korean, Chinese, Indian, and Russian backgrounds, were coded for cultural pressure toward restraint, while only three, Students E, I, and N, carried an open-expression script. For the restrained majority, performing arts often became the rare sanctioned outlet. Student L named it exactly: "Music became the one socially acceptable channel where I could be loud, dramatic, even sad in public." For students like E, the stage extended a fluency in feeling their culture had already given them.

2.1

Performance and Confidence Transfer

Performing arts are widely believed to build confidence that extends beyond the stage. The intuition has a basis in Bandura's concept of self-efficacy: succeeding at a scary task under safe conditions strengthens belief in your own abilities, and that belief can travel to other settings.

Yet the research record is split. A controlled experiment with 237 elementary students (Freeman, Sullivan & Fulton, 2003) found no significant effect of creative drama on self-concept, though the authors admitted their broad scales may have missed real changes the drama instructor saw in shy students. Meanwhile, a study of over 2,000 students (Burton, Horowitz & Abeles, 2000) found arts-rich students consistently more confident and more willing to take risks, gains that surfaced most clearly in the qualitative data, not the standardized measures.

So the disagreement may be less about whether performing builds confidence than about how confidence is measured. When studies listen to young people directly, the effect becomes visible, and our interviews found the same pattern:

2.4

Performing as Becoming Someone Else: The Mask That Freeslas dos máscaras

Several participants described a paradox: performing as someone else made it easier to be themselves. Goffman (1959) argued that everyday life is already a performance, we present a version of ourselves to an audience and worry whether it will be accepted. If ordinary life is a role, then stepping into an explicit, scripted role on stage may relieve that pressure, because the performer is no longer presenting their own self for judgment.

Drama therapists observe exactly this: under the cover of "just acting," the character becomes a shield, protection rather than disguise. Within that safe distance, adolescents can take emotional risks without feeling that they are being evaluated.

The mask has a special place in Hispanic letters. In El laberinto de la soledad (1950), Octavio Paz described the Mexican self as sheltered behind masks, the face a mask, the smile a mask, a hermeticism worn in self-defense. The adolescents in this study reveal the mask's other life: put on by choice, on a stage, it stops defending the self and starts releasing it. Between Paz's mask that hides and the theater's mask that frees runs the question at the heart of this project.

“It allows you to be on stage as someone else and not necessarily as yourself, which can be alleviation from overall judgement on yourself.”Student E · theater, verbatim
Theater as a way to express emotions she normally could not access, the character as a vehicle for feelings she would otherwise hold back.Student J

For both students, the role was not a mask that hid them but one that freed them. The character becomes a kind of permission slip, a way to feel and show what the unprotected self cannot. And for a young person taught to suppress emotion, a character may offer the rare cover under which those emotions can finally be expressed.

Qualitative research is only as trustworthy as its coding. This codebook publishes every theme used in the analysis: what it means, how many of the fifteen participants expressed it, and a representative voice where one appears in the quote bank. Tap a theme to open it.

Cultural pressure / restraint12 of 15

Definition: culture demands humility, restraint, or standards: "can't take compliments," "man up," "be humble, don't draw attention."

"In Asian cultures you have to reserve your accomplishments and joys to humble yourself and deny the compliments." (Student C, Korean)

Comparison to peers11 of 15

Definition: compares self to other performers, in ability or looks, whether the comparison felt threatening, motivating, or both.

"Comparison became information instead of verdict." (Student L)

Confidence transfer (stage to life)9 of 15

Definition: stage confidence carries into presentations and social life: "I don't fear presentations anymore," "I can start conversations now."

"Class presentations used to be my absolute worst nightmare... now I can get up there and talk without completely freezing up." (Student B)

No regrets8 of 15

Definition: believes their performing arts experience made them better and is part of their identity today.

"If I had never started singing, I honestly think I'd still be that quiet kid who avoids eye contact." (Student B)

Body as tool for expression7 of 15

Definition: reframes the body as an instrument or means of expression: "I focus on expressing, not how I look."

"Before, it was just my body. But now, it's like my body is my instrument, my tool for making music." (Student B)

Body as judged / insecurity7 of 15

Definition: feels evaluated; body-related insecurity persists despite, or within, performance.

"Because I'm skinny I don't have the power to project... when I play violin compared to other people." (Student C)

Solo as vulnerability6 of 15

Definition: solo performance or failure feels identity-threatening; solo standards pressure.

"Solo recitals are exposure, your sound and nothing else." (Student L)

Group as safety5 of 15

Definition: group performance feels safer than solo: "trusted my group, we were all in it together."

"Group performance lets you borrow courage until you grow your own." (Student L)

Definition: culture encourages emotional expression: family gatherings full of laughter, feelings named out loud.

How We Did It (Methods)

Design

Qualitative, semi-structured interviews with thematic analysis, chosen because standardized scales have missed changes performers describe in their own words.

Participants

15 adolescents and young adults: 12 performers spanning theater, choir, dance, violin, trombone, harp, piano, clarinet, and gymnastics, plus 3 non-performer controls. Backgrounds: Korean, Korean American, Chinese, Chinese American, Indian American, Russian American, Guatemalan American, and American.

Interviews

Four parts: performance history · confidence & its transfer · body and self-perception · cultural and family messages about emotion.

Analysis

Thematic coding matrix (confidence transfer, body as tool vs. judged object, cultural restraint, role distance) plus a quote bank of representative statements. The full codebook is published above.

Ethics

Informed consent for all participants; minors protected with pseudonyms (Student A-O). Real names appear nowhere in the analysis.

Limitations

Small convenience sample and a tiny comparison group, patterns are suggestive, not statistical. All data self-reported.

Conclusion: From Findings to a Proposalde los hallazgos a una propuesta

Research that ends at description leaves its best question unasked: what should anyone do? Student E named the gap directly: "The mental stress of the theater process... there's no set ways to help you not feel that way." Taken together, these findings sketch the shape of a performing-arts program for adolescents raised under scripts of restraint, built on three principles the data kept repeating:

1 · Start in groups, never solos

Group performance felt safe; solo felt exposing (Figure 3). "Group performance lets you borrow courage until you grow your own" (Student L). Build confidence in ensemble before it is ever tested alone; as L warned, solo too early can break someone.

2 · Lead with character

The role is the entry point, not the obstacle. Hand a self-conscious adolescent a character and you hand them Paz's mask turned inside out, cover under which expression finally becomes possible.

3 · Treat the cultural script as material

Restraint and simpatía are not problems to fix but scripts to perform. Let students stage their own feeling rules, and try on someone else's.

And the questions this project opens are the ones I most want to keep asking: How do bilingual adolescents switch emotional scripts along with languages? Does simpatía survive migration, or does it thin in translation? And what can the tradition of Chicano theater, Luis Valdez's El Teatro Campesino, born among striking farmworkers in 1965, with its masks and stock characters, teach about performance as a community's way of feeling out loud? Those questions are where this project ends, and where I hope my study of Hispanic literatures and cultures begins.

La Máscara: Reading Octavio Paz on a High School Stageun ensayo

Un ensayo que conecta los hallazgos de este estudio con la tradición literaria hispana que le dio al proyecto su imagen central. English version below.

En español

En "Máscaras mexicanas", el segundo ensayo de El laberinto de la soledad (1950), Octavio Paz describe un “yo” que sobrevive ocultándose. El mexicano, escribe, se encierra para preservarse: "máscara el rostro y máscara la sonrisa". Para Paz, esa reserva no es un rasgo de personalidad sino una herencia histórica, una defensa aprendida a lo largo de siglos de conquista y precariedad. Abrirse es exponerse a la herida. La máscara, en su lectura, es armadura.

Pero la psicología del mundo hispanohablante cuenta una segunda historia. La investigación sobre la simpatía describe en las culturas mexicana y latinoamericana un guión de expresión cálida y abierta, organizado en torno a compartir generosamente la emoción positiva, y los adolescentes mexicanos han reportado una satisfacción de vida superior al promedio internacional. ¿Cómo puede la cultura de la máscara ser también la cultura de la simpatía? Creo que no son una contradicción sino una coreografía. La simpatía gobierna la superficie social compartida: la calidez se ofrece hacia afuera, por diseño. La máscara protege lo interior y lo no resuelto. La emoción, en las culturas hispanas, no es simplemente "abierta". Está compuesta, puesta en escena, dotada de forma. Esa es una idea teatral, y por eso alguien que estudia teatro termina necesitando a Paz.

Mi propia investigación encontró que la máscara hace trabajos opuestos según el guión que cada persona carga. Los participantes criados bajo guiones de contención describieron el escenario como liberación: interpretar a otro les dio permiso para sentir en público por primera vez. Un participante criado en un hogar guatemalteco de expresión abierta describió lo contrario: el escenario no lo liberó de su cultura, extendió una fluidez que su cultura ya le había dado. Paz escribió la máscara que oculta. Mis participantes encontraban, una y otra vez, la máscara que liberaba. Ambas son ciertas, porque el significado de una máscara depende de quién la elige. Impuesta, encierra. Elegida, libera.

La historia del teatro hispano lo supo antes que la psicología. En 1965, sobre camiones de plataforma junto a la huelga de la uva en Delano, El Teatro Campesino de Luis Valdez convirtió las máscaras en palabra colectiva. En actos como Las dos caras del patroncito, un campesino y su patrón intercambian máscaras y, por unos minutos, vidas. Trabajadores que no podían decir sin riesgo lo que sentían podían ponerlo en escena, reírse de ello y sostenerse juntos dentro de él. La máscara elegida se volvió la manera en que una comunidad siente en voz alta.

Esta es la conversación dentro de la cual quiero pasar los próximos cuatro años: Paz y Valdez, la simpatía y el hermetismo, la literatura de una lengua que lleva cinco siglos pensando qué muestra un rostro y qué resguarda. Mi proyecto me enseñó la pregunta. Las literaturas y culturas hispánicas, leídas en mi lengua materna, son donde espero aprender sus respuestas.

English version

In "Máscaras mexicanas," the second essay of El laberinto de la soledad (1950), Octavio Paz describes a self that survives by concealment. The Mexican, he writes, closes himself off to preserve himself: "máscara el rostro y máscara la sonrisa." For Paz this guardedness is not a personality trait but a historical inheritance, a defense learned through centuries of conquest and precarity. To open yourself is to be wounded. The mask, in his account, is armor.

Yet the psychology of the Spanish-speaking world tells a second story. Research on simpatía describes in Mexican and Latin American cultures a script of warm, open expression, organized around the generous sharing of positive emotion, and Mexican adolescents have reported life satisfaction above the international average. How can the culture of the mask also be the culture of simpatía? I think the two are not a contradiction but a choreography. Simpatía governs the shared social surface: warmth is offered outward, by design. The mask guards what is interior and unresolved. Emotion in Hispanic cultures is not simply "open." It is composed, staged, given form. That is a theatrical idea, and it is why a student of performance ends up needing Paz.

My own research found the mask doing opposite work depending on the script a person carries. Participants raised under scripts of restraint described the stage as release: playing someone else gave them cover to feel in public for the first time. A participant raised in a Guatemalan household of open expression described the reverse: the stage did not free him from his culture, it extended a fluency his culture had already given him. Paz wrote the mask that hides. My participants kept finding the mask that frees. Both are true, because a mask's meaning depends on who chooses it. Imposed, it imprisons. Chosen, it liberates.

Hispanic performance history knew this before psychology did. In 1965, on flatbed trucks beside the Delano grape strike, Luis Valdez's El Teatro Campesino turned masks into collective speech. In actos like Las dos caras del patroncito, a farmworker and his boss trade masks and, for a few minutes, lives. Workers who could not safely say what they felt could stage it, laugh at it, and stand together inside it. The chosen mask became a way for a community to feel out loud.

This is the conversation I want to spend the next four years inside: Paz and Valdez, simpatía and hermetismo, the literature of a language that has spent five centuries thinking about what a face shows and what it shelters. My project taught me the question. Hispanic literatures and cultures, read in Spanish, my native language, is where I hope to learn its answers.

  • Röttger-Rössler et al. (2015), Cross-cultural childrearing: communities actively teach which emotions to express or suppress.
  • Salvador et al. (2020), Simpatía: warm, open expression of positive emotion in Latin American culture.
  • OECD PISA (2022), Korean fifteen-year-olds' life satisfaction: 6.36, ranking 66th of 73; Mexican adolescents above the OECD average in earlier rounds.

About Us 🎭

This website presents a qualitative research study on performing arts and adolescent development. Fifteen adolescents and young adults were interviewed, twelve performers in theater, dance, instrumental music, and vocal performance, plus three non-performers as a comparison group.

Resumen en español: Este proyecto examina cómo las artes escénicas ayudan a los adolescentes a ganar confianza y expresar sus emociones. A través de quince entrevistas, comparamos tres "guiones" culturales para la emoción: la calidez abierta de la simpatía latinoamericana, la expresividad individualista de los Estados Unidos y la contención de Corea. El escenario ofrece algo distinto a cada grupo: para quienes crecieron con guiones de contención, una máscara que libera; para quienes crecieron con simpatía, una extensión natural de lo que su cultura ya les enseñó a sentir.

What we asked

Semi-structured interviews covered four areas: participants' history with performance; whether confidence developed on stage carried into everyday life; how performing shaped body image and self-perception; and what messages participants' cultures and families gave them about expressing emotion.

What we found

Six of nine performers described confidence that transferred into presentations and social life, none of the three non-performers did. Many described a paradox: performing as someone else made it easier to be themselves. And a cultural thread ran through it all: participants raised with emotional restraint often found in performance a rare space where those feelings could finally be expressed.

Why these cultures

This project grew out of a particular interest in Hispanic literatures and cultures, in how Latin American values like simpatía and familismo create a shared language for emotion, set alongside the scripts of restraint that many participants described. Performance became the lens for asking what happens when those different scripts meet a stage.

That interest is ultimately literary as much as psychological: Spanish names emotional experiences, simpatía, cariño, confianza, that other languages leave unnamed, and the cultures that speak it build daily life around those words. Octavio Paz's "Máscaras mexicanas", the mask worn in self-defense, gave this project its central image turned inside out: here, the mask frees. Reading Hispanic cultures closely, through their language, their literature, and their stages, is the work this project hopes to continue.

A note on the sample

This was a small convenience sample, so the patterns here are suggestive, not statistical. All participants are identified by pseudonyms (Student A-O) to protect their privacy.

From the researcher

I came to this project as a performer first. Performing arts changed how I interact socially, they eased my shyness and made presentations and conversations far less intimidating, and I wanted to know whether that change was real, whether others felt it, and how much of it culture writes for us. Fifteen conversations later, my answer: it is real, it is shared, and the shape a culture gives it is the most interesting part. Spanish is my native language; part of this project was turning the culture I feel at home in into a question I could study. Si Ah Jang
